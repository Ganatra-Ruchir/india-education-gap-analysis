How well is each Indian state teaching its kids to read and write?

A simple, state-by-state look at literacy (who can read and write) and the gap between boys and girls — using real government school records from 2015–16, not guesses or made-up numbers.

35 states & union territories · one snapshot in time · every chart below is interactive — just hover over it

Boys vs. girls

In every single state, boys are ahead of girls

This chart compares literacy for boys (blue) and girls (pink) in each state. In every state, boys score higher — the only question is by how much. In Rajasthan, the gap is the biggest in the country: almost 28 points. Hover over the dots to compare a state's boys and girls directly.

States are sorted from smallest gap to biggest · the 3 widest gaps are labeled
Rajasthan, Jharkhand, and Chhattisgarh have the biggest gaps between boys and girls — more than double the gap seen in states like Meghalaya or Mizoram.
Connecting the dots

The more literate a state is, the smaller its boy-girl gap tends to be

Here, each dot is one state. States further to the right have higher overall literacy. States lower down have a smaller gap between boys and girls. The pattern is clear: better overall literacy usually comes with a smaller gap — these two problems seem to move together, not separately.

Each dot is one state · the red line shows the overall pattern
This is a strong pattern (measured as −0.74 on a scale from −1 to 1, where numbers closer to −1 or 1 mean a stronger link). In plain terms: raising overall literacy and closing the gender gap look like the same fight, not two separate ones.
Testing a common assumption

Bigger classrooms don't clearly mean worse exam results

It seems obvious: more students per teacher should mean worse exam scores. This data says that's not really true. Uttar Pradesh and Bihar have the most crowded classrooms in the country, but their pass rates land right in the middle of the pack — not at the bottom.

Each dot is one state · the flat line shows there's basically no pattern here
This measured at almost 0 (−0.13 on the same −1 to 1 scale) — in plain terms, barely any link at all. It would have been easy to quietly drop this chart when it didn't confirm the expected story. It's shown here anyway: finding out that a popular assumption doesn't hold up is just as useful as confirming one that does, especially before spending a real budget on it.

What this data can't tell us

Being upfront about the limits

  • This is one snapshot in time (2015-16), not a multi-year trend — it shows how things looked, not how they're changing.
  • Telangana isn't included — it split off from Andhra Pradesh in 2014, and its literacy numbers weren't reported separately yet in this data. Left out on purpose, not filled in with a guess.
  • Delhi's exam pass rate is shown as missing, not zero — the records show no students appeared for that exam that year, which isn't the same as everyone failing.
  • 35 of India's 36 states and union territories are included, after cleaning up a real government file with over 600 columns.
